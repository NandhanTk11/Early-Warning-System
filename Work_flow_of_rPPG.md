The workflow is structured into four main phases:​
1. Data Pre-processing & ROI Extraction
●​ Frame Enhancement: Input video frames are converted to grayscale and enhanced using Contrast
Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to normalize variations in low-light conditions.
●​ Face & ROI Detection: A Haar feature-based cascade classifier detects the facial boundary. The
system dynamically extracts three primary regions of interest (ROIs)—the forehead, left cheek, and
right cheek—and splits them into smaller sub-ROIs to mitigate the effects of uneven lighting and facial
expressions.
●​ Raw Signal Extraction: Raw rPPG time-series traces are generated by taking spatial averages of pixel
intensities, primarily focusing on the green color channel due to its high absorption sensitivity to
hemoglobin.
2. Multi-Stage Signal Denoising
●​ Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT): The raw RGB trace is decomposed into multi-resolution wavelet
components7. Applying Donoho and Johnstone's universal thresholding rule based on Median
Absolute Deviation (MAD), high-frequency noise coefficients are suppressed while cardiac pulse
waveforms are preserved during Inverse DWT reconstruction7more_horiz.
●​ Dual-Stage Bandpass Filtering:
○​ Stage 1 (Wide Passband: 0.6–3.5 Hz): Eliminates slow baseline drifts, head motion wander,
and high-frequency sensor noise10.
○​ Stage 2 (Narrow Passband: 0.8–2.5 Hz): Targets dominant heart rate frequencies to isolate
the cardiac signal from motion harmonics.
3. Residual-Based Adaptive Kalman Filtering (RAKF)
●​ Hidden State Modeling: Reference HR is modeled as a hidden state evolving through a random walk
process.
●​ Adaptive Noise Covariance: Rather than using a static measurement noise covariance, RAKF
dynamically adjusts $R_{\text{adaptive}}$) frame-by-frame based on the magnitude of the innovation
residual.
●​ Outlier Suppression: Sudden illumination or motion spikes are rejected by comparing current
observations against a local median historical window.
●​ Signal Quality Index (SQ) Weighting: State updates are moderated by a dynamic weight factor $w_k
= \max(\alpha, SQ_k)$. The Signal Quality Index (SQ) normalizes and combines Spectral Power
Ratio (0.3), Signal-to-Noise Ratio (0.4), and Peak Stability (0.3) to prevent low-quality frames from
corrupting state estimates18.

4. Dual-Domain Estimation & Weighted Fusion
●​ Time-Domain Analysis: Systolic peaks are identified within a physiological frequency band of 0.75–2.5
Hz (45–150 bpm) to compute inter-beat intervals.
●​ Frequency-Domain Analysis: Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) spectral peak detection is conducted on
the filtered rPPG signal.
●​ Weighted Fusion Scheme: Estimates from both domains are combined using a quality-guided
weighted fusion scheme, giving higher weight to the domain with the superior Signal Quality Index to
produce the final, temporally consistent heart rate estimate.

The Data Preprocessing and Region of Interest (ROI) Detection stage converts raw camera video input into
high-quality, normalized temporal color traces suitable for physiological signal extraction[1][2]. This process
consists of five distinct steps:

1. Video Frame Standardization & Resolution Handling
●​ Pixel Density Preservation: High pixel density and resolution are maintained across video frames rather
than downsampling, as higher resolution captures subtle micro-vascular skin color variations more
effectively[2].
●​ Grayscale Transformation: For initial face boundary detection, each RGB frame is transformed into a
grayscale representation to streamline computational feature extraction[2].
2. Illumination Normalization via CLAHE
●​ Brightness Assessment: System checks frame-by-frame luminance levels to detect low-light or uneven
lighting conditions[2].
●​ CLAHE Enhancement: Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is applied to
normalize contrast variations across the facial surface, preventing local lighting imbalances or
shadows from obscuring the underlying pulsatile signal[2].
3. Facial Boundary Detection
●​ Haar Cascade Classifier: The framework employs a Haar feature-based cascade classifier to detect
and track facial boundaries within the video frame reliably across frames[2].
4. Dynamic ROI Selection & Sub-ROI Division
●​ Primary ROI Locations: Once the face boundary is established, the algorithm dynamically identifies
three key facial skin regions[2]:
1.​ Forehead
2.​ Left Cheek
3.​ Right Cheek
●​ Sub-ROI Segmentation: Each detected primary ROI is further subdivided into smaller sub-ROIs[2][3].
●​ Purpose: Segmenting into smaller sub-ROIs reduces signal degradation caused by non-uniform facial
illumination and minimizes noise introduced by subtle facial movements or expressions (e.g., smiling,
talking)[3].

5. Color Channel Extraction & Spatial Averaging
●​ Green Channel Prioritization: Spatial pixel values are extracted across Red, Green, and Blue
channels ($c \in \{r, g, b\}$), with the green channel ($g(t)$) receiving primary emphasis because
hemoglobin exhibits peak light absorption sensitivity within the green wavelength spectrum.

1. Conceptual Breakdown of CLAHE
Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is an advanced digital image-processing
technique used to enhance contrast in images, particularly under non-uniform or low-light conditions.
●​ Standard Histogram Equalization (HE): Computes a single global contrast adjustment for an entire
image. While simple, it often fails when an image contains both very dark and bright areas, causing
over-exposure or loss of detail in local regions.
●​ Adaptive Histogram Equalization (AHE): Solves the global limitation by dividing the image into smaller
contextual regions (called tiles or sub-blocks) and applying histogram equalization locally. However,
AHE tends to over-amplify background noise in relatively uniform regions.
●​ Contrast Limited (CLAHE): Addresses the noise problem of AHE by introducing a clip limit (contrast
threshold). Any histogram bin that exceeds this clip limit is truncated, and the clipped pixels are
redistributed uniformly across the histogram before performing equalization. This enhances local
contrast and edge detail without magnifying noise or creating artificial glare.
2. Utilization in the Proposed Methodology
In the DWT-RAKF rPPG pipeline, CLAHE is deployed during the initial data pre-processing stage:
●​ Frame Luminance Assessment: After standardizing video frames and converting them into grayscale
images, the system assesses frame-by-frame brightness levels[1].
●​ Mitigating Ambient & Low-Light Variations: Recorded facial video sequences frequently experience
natural lighting fluctuations, producing shaded and lit regions across the face[2]. CLAHE is applied to
manage low-light conditions and normalize contrast across unevenly illuminated facial areas[1].
●​ Facial Detection & Sub-ROI Preparation: By stabilizing local contrast and suppressing lighting
glare/shadows, CLAHE ensures that the subsequent Haar feature-based cascade classifier can reliably
detect facial boundaries[1]. This contrast enhancement also protects downstream forehead and cheek
sub-ROI extraction from signal degradation caused by uneven illumination[1][3].

What is Grayscale Transformation?
Grayscale transformation is an image-processing operation that converts a multi-channel color image (such as
a 3-channel RGB image containing red, green, and blue color values per pixel) into a single-channel intensity
image. In a grayscale image, each pixel carries only a luminance value representing shades of gray ranging

from black (0 intensity) to white (255 intensity), eliminating color hue and saturation while preserving structural
geometry, contrast, and edge details.
Why It Is Done in This Methodology
In the DWT-RAKF framework, transforming video frames into grayscale is a critical step within the data
pre-processing and face detection pipeline for several key reasons[1]:
1.​ Required Input for Haar Cascade Face Detection: Following initial contrast enhancement via CLAHE,
the system converts video frames to grayscale to feed them into a Haar feature-based cascade
classifier[1]. Haar cascade classifiers rely on calculating intensity differences between adjacent
rectangular regions (detecting structural features like edges and line contrasts around the eyes, nose,
and mouth) rather than color information[1].
2.​ Computational Efficiency & Speed: Processing full 3-channel RGB video frames for facial tracking in
every frame adds unnecessary computational overhead. By reducing the data representation from three
color dimensions (RGB) down to a single intensity dimension, the algorithm significantly reduces
memory footprint and speeds up real-time facial boundary detection[1].
3.​ Prerequisite for Dynamic ROI Localization: Grayscale conversion serves specifically to localize facial
boundaries reliably[1]. Once the face box is accurately established, the framework dynamically defines
the primary Regions of Interest (forehead, left cheek, right cheek) and accesses the original color
channels to compute the green-channel temporal traces needed for physiological pulse extraction[1][2].

2.​ Sub-ROI Coordinate Calculation: Within that bounding box, the framework calculates the bounding
spatial coordinates for the three primary regions of interest (forehead, left cheek, and right cheek) and
their smaller sub-ROIs.
3.​ Defining the Coordinate Set: This produces a set of 2D spatial coordinates defined mathematically as
$\text{ROI} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, representing all valid pixel row/column indices $(i, j)$ belonging to

●​ The spatial coordinate set $(i, j) \in \text{ROI}$ derived from the grayscale frame is applied directly as a

●​ Diffuse vs. Specular Reflection: Camera sensors record both specular (surface reflection) and diffuse

2. Mathematical Formulation & Spatial Averaging
To reduce high-frequency spatial noise and camera sensor quantization errors, pixel intensities across the
detected facial skin regions are spatially averaged for each frame.
●​ ROI Definition: Let $\text{ROI} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ represent the set of 2D spatial coordinates for
the selected facial regions (the forehead, left cheek, and right cheek sub-ROIs).
●​ Pixel Intensity: Let $I_c(i, j, t)$ denote the intensity of a pixel at spatial coordinates $(i, j)$ at frame time
$t$ for a specific color channel $c \in \{r, g, b\}$.
●​ Spatial Averaging Equation: The temporal color signal $g(t)$ is calculated as the mean pixel intensity
across all $N$ pixels within the defined ROI:
$$g(t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{(i, j) \in \text{ROI}} I_c(i, j, t)$$
where $N$ is the total number of pixels in the selected ROI.

In the proposed framework, Signal Denoising through Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) serves as the
multi-resolution noise-filtering stage to clean the raw, noisy photoplethysmography (rPPG) trace $g(t)$
extracted from video frames.
Conventional filtering often struggles with transient motion artifacts because fixed cutoffs can corrupt
non-stationary biological signals. DWT overcomes this by representing the signal across multiple
time-frequency resolution scales, allowing the framework to isolate high-frequency noise from physiological
pulse dynamics.
1. Linear Wavelet Decomposition
The raw PPG signal $g(t)$ is represented mathematically as a linear combination of scaled and shifted wavelet
basis functions $\psi_{j,k}(t)$:
$$g(t) = \sum_{j=0}^{J} \sum_{k} c_{j,k} \, \psi_{j,k}(t)$$
Where:
●​ $c_{j,k}$ are the wavelet coefficients at scale $j$ and time position $k$.
●​ $\psi_{j,k}(t)$ is the mother wavelet basis function.
●​ Scale Index $j$: Refers to the decomposition level. Lower values of $j$ capture high-frequency details
(typically sensor noise and high-frequency motion), while higher values of $j$ capture lower-frequency
components (slowly varying trends).
●​ Translation Index $k$: Localizes the position of wavelets in time, enabling the isolation of transient
noise spikes.
●​ Maximum Decomposition Depth $J$: Sets the maximum decomposition level to capture the target
heart rate frequency band.
2. Universal Noise Threshold Calculation (MAD Rule)
To shrink noise-dominated wavelet coefficients without manual parameter tuning, the framework uses Donoho
and Johnstone's Universal Thresholding Rule. The noise variance $\sigma$ is estimated using the Median
Absolute Deviation (MAD) of the finest-scale detail coefficients ($j=1$).
The universal threshold $T$ is computed as:
$$T = \sigma \sqrt{2 \log n}$$
Where:
●​ $n$ is the total signal length.
●​ $\sigma$ is the MAD estimate of noise from the first-level detail coefficients.
●​ This data-driven threshold selection is mathematically optimal because it minimizes the mean squared
error (MSE) of signal reconstruction under Gaussian noise.
3. Soft Thresholding (Coefficient Shrinkage)
Once threshold $T$ is calculated, soft thresholding is applied to detail coefficients $d_{j,k}$ across
decomposition levels:

## Page 9

$$\tilde{d}_{j,k} = \begin{cases} \text{sgn}(d_{j,k}) \cdot (|d_{j,k}| - T), & \text{if } |d_{j,k}| > T \\ 0, &
\text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
Where:
●​ $d_{j,k}$ represents the raw detail coefficients at scale $j$.
●​ $\text{sgn}(\cdot)$ is the signum function.
●​ $\tilde{d}_{j,k}$ are the shrunk, denoised detail coefficients.
Mechanism: Coefficients smaller than threshold $T$ (associated with random noise) are set to zero, while
coefficients larger than $T$ are smoothly shrunken. This attenuates unwanted noise while preserving
significant physiological oscillations caused by arterial blood volume changes.
4. Signal Reconstruction via Inverse DWT (IDWT)
After suppressing noisy coefficients, the clean time-domain rPPG signal $\hat{g}(t)$ is reconstructed by taking
the Inverse Discrete Wavelet Transform (IDWT) using the modified coefficients $\tilde{c}_{j,k}$:
$$\hat{g}(t) = \sum_{j=0}^{J} \sum_{k} \tilde{c}_{j,k} \, \psi_{j,k}(t)$$
This denoised output $\hat{g}(t)$ exhibits a smooth quasi-sinusoidal cardiac waveform and is subsequently
passed to the dual-stage bandpass filter and Residual-based Adaptive Kalman Filter (RAKF) for further
processing.

Here is a breakdown of what each part means in plain language:
1. "Data-Driven Threshold Selection"
●​ The Concept: Instead of manually picking or hardcoding a fixed cutoff number (which might work for
one video but fail for another), the threshold value $T$ is calculated automatically from the signal itsel.
●​ How It Works: It uses the signal's sample length $n$ and estimates the actual noise intensity $\sigma$
directly from the finest-scale wavelet detail coefficients using Median Absolute Deviation (MAD).
2. "Under Gaussian Noise Conditions"
●​ The Concept: Random background interference—such as camera sensor noise, mild illumination jitter,
or thermal fluctuations—typically follows a Gaussian (bell-curve) normal distribution.
●​ Why It Matters: Knowing that the random sensor noise behaves like a normal distribution allows
mathematicians to predict how high random noise spikes will typically reach across $n$ samples.
3. "Minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE)"
●​ The Concept: Mean Squared Error (MSE) measures the average squared difference between the
cleaned, reconstructed signal and the true, underlying cardiac pulse wave.

## Page 10

●​ Why It Matters: Minimizing MSE means that after removing the noise, the resulting waveform is as
close to the true biological heart rate signal as mathematically possible, without distorting its shape.
4. "Mathematically Optimal"
●​ The Concept: In statistical signal processing, Donoho and Johnstone proved that setting the threshold
to $T = \sigma \sqrt{2 \log n}$ guarantees that with high probability, the maximum noise value across
$n$ samples will not exceed $T$.
●​ The Balance: It hits the sweet spot between two common filtering traps:
○​ Under-filtering: Setting $T$ too low leaves noise bumps behind that look like false heartbeats.
○​ Over-smoothing: Setting $T$ too high chops off real systolic pulse peaks, destroying vital heart
rate data.
Summary in Practical Terms
In the context of remote heart rate measurement, this means the algorithm automatically calculates a custom
noise cutoff for each video trace that removes as much camera sensor noise as possible while preserving the
true blood volume pulse peaks.

Following the multi-resolution denoising performed by the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), the framework
applies a dual-stage bandpass filtering strategy. This two-tiered filtering process sequentially isolates cardiac
frequencies while stripping away non-cardiac interference, baseline drift, and motion harmonics.
1. Stage 1: Wide Bandpass Filtering (0.6 – 3.5 Hz)
●​ Filter Specification: Implemented using a Butterworth bandpass filter2.
●​ Passband Range: 0.6 Hz to 3.5 Hz (corresponding to a broad heart rate spectrum of 36 BPM to 210
BPM).
●​ Primary Target Noise:
○​ Low-Frequency Noise: Eliminates slow baseline wander, head movement drift, and gradual
ambient lighting changes.
○​ High-Frequency Noise: Attenuates camera sensor noise and quantization artifacts.
●​ Function & Role: rPPG signals are highly susceptible to illumination shifts and movement fluctuations,
which cause severe pulse distortion and spectral leakage if left untreated2. Stage 1 stabilizes the signal
baseline while preserving the full physiological range of human cardiac activity, producing a clean,
refined trace for time-domain analysis.
2. Stage 2: Narrow Bandpass Filtering (0.8 – 2.5 Hz)
●​ Passband Range: 0.8 Hz to 2.5 Hz (focusing strictly on 48 BPM to 150 BPM).
●​ Primary Target Noise: Motion harmonics, residual motion artifacts (MAs), and ambient noise outside
the typical human resting/light-activity heart rate band.
●​ Function & Role: By narrowing the passband directly around the most dominant cardiac frequencies,
Stage 2 isolates the principal blood volume pulse3. This prevents secondary motion harmonics from
creating false spectral peaks in downstream analyses.

Raw camera video traces contain large, high-amplitude interference from slow baseline drift, head

If a narrow filter (0.8–2.5 Hz) is applied directly to a noisy raw signal, the large out-of-band noise spikes can

The Residual-Based Adaptive Kalman Filter (RAKF) is designed to track a person's heart rate frame-by-frame
from video data while automatically handling motion spikes, camera noise, and lighting shifts.
Standard Kalman filters assume that sensor noise stays constant over time; however, video camera quality
fluctuates continuously due to head movements and ambient light shifts. RAKF solves this by using innovation
residuals and a Signal Quality Index (SQ) to dynamically adjust its calculations.

1. State-Space Representation (Modeling the Heart Rate)
The true underlying heart rate \(x_k\) is modeled as a hidden state that evolves smoothly over time, while
\(z_k\) represents the pulse rate observed from the video signal.
\[x_k = x_{k-1} + w_{k-1}, \quad w_{k-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_k)\] \[z_k = x_k + v_k, \quad v_k \sim
\mathcal{N}(0, R_k)\]
●​ \(x_k\): The actual hidden heart rate at video frame \(k\).
●​ \(z_k\): The observed pulse rate derived from spectral analysis of the filtered video trace at frame \(k\).
●​ \(w_{k-1}\) & \(v_k\): Random process noise and measurement noise, assumed to follow Gaussian
distributions with covariances \(Q_k\) and \(R_k\) respectively.
●​ Intuition: Your body's true heart rate (\(x_k\)) changes gradually from one frame to the next, while the
camera's estimate (\(z_k\)) contains measurement noise.
2. Prediction Step (Prior Estimate)
Before evaluating the new video frame, the filter predicts the current heart rate (\(\hat{x}_k^-\)) and updates its
error covariance (\(P_k^-\)) based on the previous frame:
\[\hat{x}k^- = \hat{x}{k-1}\] \[P_k^- = P_{k-1} + Q_k\]
●​ \(\hat{x}_k^-\): The prior estimate of the heart rate before looking at frame \(k\).
●​ \(P_k^-\): The prior estimation uncertainty.
●​ Intuition: Assuming no sudden physical exertion, your heart rate in the current millisecond is expected
to be roughly identical to where it was a fraction of a second ago.

## Page 14

3. Innovation Residual (Measuring Discrepancy)
The residual \(\tilde{y}_k\) measures the discrepancy between the new camera observation \(z_k\) and the
prior predicted state \(\hat{x}_k^-\):
\[\tilde{y}_k = z_k - \hat{x}_k^-\]
●​ Intuition: If the model predicts 70 BPM but the camera suddenly reads 110 BPM, the residual
\(\tilde{y}_k = +40 \text{ BPM}\) signals a major discrepancy.
4. Adaptive Measurement Noise Covariance
Rather than keeping measurement noise fixed, RAKF dynamically increases the noise covariance
\(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\) whenever the residual \(|\tilde{y}_k|\) surges:
\[R_{\text{adaptive}, k} = R_0 \left( 1 + \frac{|\tilde{y}_k|}{\beta} \right)\]
●​ \(R_0\): The basal measurement noise covariance (set empirically to 25 BPM).
●​ \(\beta > 0\): A sensitivity parameter controlling responsiveness to residual spikes.
●​ Intuition: If a sudden head movement causes a large jump in the camera reading, the residual
\(|\tilde{y}k|\) spikes. The filter reacts by penalizing the reading with a high noise value
\(R{\text{adaptive}, k}\), effectively marking the frame as untrustworthy.
5. Kalman Gain Computation (Trust Weighting)
The Kalman gain \(K_k\) acts as a blending factor (ranging between 0 and 1) that balances the model
prediction against the camera reading:
\[K_k = \frac{P_k^-}{P_k^- + R_{\text{adaptive}, k}}\]
●​ Clean Signal (Low Residual): \(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\) stays low, making \(K_k\) higher so the filter
trusts the incoming camera reading.
●​ Noisy Signal (High Residual): \(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\) spikes, driving \(K_k\) close to 0 so the filter
ignores the bad reading and relies on its prior prediction.
6. Outlier Handling via Local Reference
To prevent extreme illumination flashes or sudden physical jerks from corrupting updates, observation \(z_k\)
is checked against the median \(\bar{z}_k\) of previous observations:
\[\text{if } |z_k - \bar{z}k| > T{\text{out}}, \quad \text{then } z_k \leftarrow \bar{z}_k\]
●​ \(T_{\text{out}}\): A predefined threshold based on standard deviations of historical residuals.
●​ Intuition: If a single frame produces an unrealistic spike, it is replaced by the local median before the
state update is calculated.
7. State Update with Dynamic Signal Quality (SQ) Weighting

## Page 15

Statistical residuals alone do not always capture physical video artifacts. Therefore, RAKF multiplies the state
correction by a dynamic weight \(w_k\) derived from physical signal quality features:
\[SQ_k = 0.3 \cdot \text{SPR} + 0.4 \cdot \text{SNR} + 0.3 \cdot \text{PS}\] \[w_k = \max(\alpha, SQ_k)\]
\[\hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^- + K_k \cdot w_k \cdot (z_k - \hat{x}_k^-)\]
●​ \(SQ_k\): The Signal Quality Index combining Spectral Power Ratio (SPR), Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR),
and Peak Stability (PS).
●​ \(\alpha\): Minimum weight threshold (between 0.1 and 0.6) to allow continuous tracking even during
low-quality intervals.
●​ \(\hat{x}_k\): The final updated posterior heart rate estimate for frame \(k\).
8. Error Covariance Update
Finally, the posterior estimation error covariance \(P_k\) is updated for the next frame:
\[P_k = (1 - K_k) P_k^-\]
●​ Consistency Note: The physical signal quality weight \(w_k\) is deliberately excluded from this
covariance equation to preserve statistical consistency under Gaussian noise propagation.

In the context of probability, statistics, and the Kalman Filter (RAKF) used in this paper, covariance is a
mathematical measure of uncertainty or variance surrounding a variable's estimated value.
When the paper refers to covariance terms—such as process noise covariance, measurement noise
covariance, and estimation error covariance—it is quantifying how much the system trusts its internal
predictions versus how much it trusts the video camera readings.
1. The Four Covariance Terms in RAKF
A. Process Noise Covariance (\(Q_k\))
●​ What it means: The uncertainty associated with how much a person's actual heart rate can naturally
change from one frame to the next.
●​ Paper's context: The paper sets this to a small fixed value (\(Q_k = 2 \times 10^{-4}\)) to reflect that a
person's true physiological heart rate changes smoothly over fractions of a second under random walk
assumptions.
B. Measurement Noise Covariance (\(R_k\) and \(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\))
●​ What it means: The uncertainty or expected noise present in the video camera's reading.
●​ Paper's context: In standard Kalman filters, \(R_k\) is assumed to be constant. In RAKF, the paper
dynamically updates this to \(R_{\text{adaptive}, k} = R_0 \left( 1 + \frac{|\tilde{y}_k|}{\beta} \right)\)
based on frame-wise residuals (\(\tilde{y}_k\)).
○​ Low Residual (Clean Video): \(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\) remains small, meaning low noise/high
certainty in the camera reading.

## Page 16

○​ High Residual (Head Movement/Lighting Shift): \(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\) spikes, signaling that
the camera reading is corrupted by noise and should not be trusted.
C. Prior Estimation Error Covariance (\(P_k^-\))
●​ What it means: The uncertainty in the predicted heart rate state before looking at the new video frame.
●​ Paper's context: It is calculated as \(P_k^- = P_{k-1} + Q_k\), accumulating the previous uncertainty plus
the small process noise.
D. Posterior Estimation Error Covariance (\(P_k\))
●​ What it means: The remaining uncertainty in the final updated heart rate estimate after combining the
model prediction with the new camera observation.
●​ Paper's context: Updated via \(P_k = (1 - K_k) P_k^-\), reflecting probabilistic uncertainty propagation
based on Gaussian noise definitions.
2. How Covariance Controls Filter "Trust"
All of these covariance terms come together in the Kalman Gain equation:
\[K_k = \frac{P_k^-}{P_k^- + R_{\text{adaptive}, k}}\]
●​ If Measurement Covariance (\(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\)) is large (high noise), the denominator grows,
making \(K_k \approx 0\). The filter ignores the bad camera frame and relies on its prior state
prediction.
●​ If Measurement Covariance (\(R_{\text{adaptive}, k}\)) is small (low noise), \(K_k \approx 1\). The filter
heavily relies on the incoming video frame to update its estimate.

In the DWT-RAKF framework, Peak Detection and Heart Rate (HR) Measurement is the final stage where the
cleaned, filtered photoplethysmography waveform \(\hat{s}(t)\) is converted into an accurate heart rate value
in Beats Per Minute (BPM).
To maximize accuracy and resilience against noise, the system uses a dual-domain approach that combines
time-domain systolic peak detection with frequency-domain spectral analysis through a quality-weighted
fusion scheme.
1. Time-Domain Peak Detection & Inter-Beat Interval (IBI)
A. Identifying Systolic Peaks
The algorithm scans the filtered time-series rPPG signal \(\hat{s}(t)\) to locate the temporal positions of
consecutive systolic peaks. Let \({t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N}\) represent the timestamps of \(N\) detected peaks over
a signal duration \(T\).
To prevent false peak detections from residual noise or motion artifacts, the peak search is constrained within
a physiological heart rate frequency range of 0.75 Hz to 2.5 Hz (45 to 150 BPM). Candidate peaks are
evaluated using established signal-processing criteria:

## Page 17

●​ Peak Distance: Enforces a minimum time gap between peaks based on human refractory periods.
●​ Peak Prominence: Measures how much a peak stands out relative to its local baseline.
●​ Peak Width: Validates the shape of the systolic pulse wave.
B. Inter-Peak Interval (\(\Delta t_i\)) & Average IBI (\(\Delta t\))
Once valid peak timestamps \({t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N}\) are established, the individual time interval \(\Delta t_i\)
between each pair of adjacent peaks is calculated as:
\[\Delta t_i = t_{i+1} - t_i, \quad \text{for } i = 1, \dots, N-1 \quad\]
The average Inter-Beat Interval (IBI), denoted as \(\Delta t\), is then computed across all \(N-1\) intervals:
\[\Delta t = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \Delta t_i \quad\]
C. Time-Domain HR Calculation
From the average IBI (\(\Delta t\)), the peak heartbeat frequency \(f_{\text{peak}}\) (in Hz or beats per second)
is derived as:
\[f_{\text{peak}} = \frac{1}{\Delta t} \quad\]
Multiplying \(f_{\text{peak}}\) by 60 converts the frequency into the final time-domain Heart Rate in BPM:
\[\text{HR (BPM)} = 60 \times f_{\text{peak}} = 60 \times \frac{1}{\Delta t} \quad\]
2. Frequency-Domain (Spectral) HR Estimation
In parallel with time-domain peak detection, the framework computes a frequency-domain HR estimate.
●​ A Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is applied to the filtered rPPG signal \(\hat{s}(t)\) to obtain its
frequency power spectrum \(S(f) = \mathcal{F}{\hat{s}(t)}\).
●​ The dominant spectral power peak within the physiological band (0.75–2.5 Hz) is identified to produce
the spectral-domain pulse rate observation
3. Quality-Aware Weighted Fusion Scheme
Rather than relying solely on time-domain peak counting (which can miss peaks during motion) or
frequency-domain FFT (which can suffer from windowing delays), the framework merges both estimates:
1.​ Signal Quality Weighting: The time-domain estimate and spectral-domain estimate are each evaluated
using the frame's Signal Quality Index (\(SQ\)).
2.​ Dynamic Fusion: The estimate with the higher \(SQ\) score is assigned a proportionally larger weight in
a weighted fusion formula.
3.​ Temporal Consistency: This quality-guided weighting ensures that if motion briefly degrades
time-domain peak shapes, the system automatically relies more heavily on the frequency spectrum
(and vice-versa), maintaining stable frame-by-frame measurement.
